At a glance

  • Diaspora engagement: The Commission on Filipinos Overseas (CFO) described services for migrants and programs that connect overseas Filipinos and younger generations with Philippine communities, culture and development. Senators supported a broader diaspora focus and asked how programs could encourage people to return and contribute.
  • Exhibition center and development: The Philippine Reclamation Authority (PRA) presented the Philippine International Exhibition Center (PIEC) as a convention, exhibition and trade venue within a larger coastal development supported by private-sector infrastructure.   • Reclamation oversight: Questions examined when PRA approval is required, whether public works meet the reclamation definition, and how PRA and the Laguna Lake Development Authority (LLDA) share authority. Members challenged differences in enforcement around Taguig and the Laguna Lakeshore Road Network; the competing jurisdictional interpretations remained unresolved.
  • Follow-up and budget status: The PRA agreed to a coordination meeting involving relevant agencies and local participants. Both agency budgets remained subject to further discussion and requested submissions for consideration; no appropriation was approved in the hearing.

Asked whether the 2027 amount would prevent delay, Lopez said yes; if funds fell short, the PRA would borrow and repay from the 2028 budget. He also said final billing would be retained until completion.

Commission on Filipinos Overseas

Diaspora engagement mandate and programs: Ang described the CFO’s mandate under Batas Pambansa 79 as fostering diaspora engagement and connecting non-resident Filipinos (NRFs) to the Philippines politically, economically, and culturally so they can participate in national development. He described NRFs as including permanent migrants, expatriates, dual citizens, marriage migrants, their descendants, and cultural-exchange participants. He said the CFO’s programs support contributions and skills transfer to communities, including through medical missions, capacity-building, consultations, and Lingkod sa Kapwa Pilipino (LINKAPIL)’s support for small and medium enterprises and small-scale infrastructure. He also described NRFs as informal ambassadors and said the CFO works with diaspora figures to open business, social, and tourism opportunities.

01:13:30 · asr-00022001:13:55 · asr-000224

Program priorities and planned work: Ang said the CFO would continue BaLinkBayan, YouLeaD, and Ugnayan while intensifying diaspora engagement. Planned work included outreach to overseas Filipino organizations, youth development modules, stronger anti-human-trafficking efforts, and partnerships with migration and development stakeholders. The presentation video described LINKAPIL as a channel for diaspora support to local development; BaLinkBayan links diaspora participation with local needs; YouLeaD and the Virtual Centro Rizal engage younger generations; Ugnayan brings services to Filipino communities overseas; and CFO programs also support migration preparation, counseling, exchange visitors, and anti-trafficking coordination.

The presentation reported more than P3.4 billion in LINKAPIL contributions over the decades and P14.4 million in 2025 donations from 2,835 overseas donors, benefiting 17,698 Filipinos across 17 provinces and the National Capital Region. It counted 81,859 migrants and program participants in 2025 across pre-departure, counseling, au pair and exchange-visitor programs. PESO Sense provides financial literacy through a digital community, while upgraded information systems and honorary diaspora representatives are intended to extend access overseas.

Philippine Reclamation Authority and PIEC presentation

PIEC’s tourism and convention case: Lopez said the PIEC would be a 140,000-square-meter exhibition and convention venue intended to attract meetings, incentives, conventions and exhibitions (MICE), trade fairs, and association congresses that existing facilities could not accommodate. He contrasted the cited capacities of current venues with the PIEC’s scale and said the project could attract an additional 300,000 to 500,000 delegates at a 50–60% utilization rate. He also compared its prospects with Singapore’s trade expo, which he said had a 120,000-square-meter area and attracted 1.5 million participants.

On visitor spending, Lopez said a regular tourist spends P6,000 per night or day, while a business-event tourist brings in P30,000 because a corporation or government funds the visit and the tourist sets up booths. He argued that the PIEC could generate activity across tourism, commerce, real estate, transport, services, hotels, shopping, and restaurants, and act as an anchor development attracting hotel owners. He said PRA wanted to draw some MICE tourism from Singapore, which he cited as having 1.5 million MICE participants at Singapore Expo.

01:47:30 · asr-00080301:48:30 · asr-00080501:49:00 · asr-000806

Pasay Eco-City development and private-sector infrastructure: Lopez located the PIEC on 81,000 square meters of PRA-owned land within the Pasay City Harbor Reclamation, part of a roughly 6.5-million-square-meter Pasay Eco-City coastal development. He described the broader development as advancing alongside the PIEC, with private-sector funding for bridges and infrastructure. He said 15 bridges would connect the area to the mainland without government expense; the network would include roads, sewer and flood-control lines, revetment, bike lanes, sidewalks, and utility lines. He also described parks, green space, and a new coastline as free to government and funded by private-sector partners.

Lopez said construction had accelerated and that seven bridges were expected to be completed before the PIEC’s target completion date. He presented the PIEC as an anchor for jobs, investment, and development in the surrounding area.

Senator Alan Peter S. Cayetano

Overseas Filipinos and a broader mandate: Cayetano recalled that his original legislative proposal was for a department for overseas Filipinos, rather than the Department of Migrant Workers (DMW). Drawing on an encounter with young people of Filipino heritage in the United Kingdom who had never visited the Philippines, he argued that citizenship alone should not determine whether their Filipino roots merit engagement. He noted that dual citizenship may offer little incentive to some families abroad.

With the Department of Migrant Workers law set to trigger a review early the following year, he urged the Cabinet to consider how the Department of Migrant Workers, the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) and the Commission on Filipinos Overseas (CFO) would coordinate as the government considers Filipinos overseas beyond migrant workers. He praised pre-departure orientation seminars and related programs, while urging complementary programs abroad that help younger people of Filipino origin learn about the Philippines.

Diaspora ties, heritage visits and tourism: Cayetano urged the CFO to build programs that connect people of Filipino heritage with the country through visits, cultural exchange and sustained relationships. He cited a figure of more than 10 million Filipinos abroad and recalled a Department of Foreign Affairs estimate of 6 million people in the United States with Filipino heritage. As an illustrative scenario, he said that if 10% traveled to the Philippines, they would number 600,000; including one non-Filipino-descent spouse or child each would make 1.2 million visitors. He compared that scenario with what he described as a tourism high of around 1.6 million visitors, and said every two to three tourists create one Filipino job. He pointed to Filipino food, ube, hilot and family-oriented culture as possible sources of wider cultural interest, and suggested programs for people of Filipino heritage to visit, study, spend a summer, start a business or retire in the Philippines.

He also encouraged sustained links with officials and community leaders of Filipino heritage overseas, using personal relationships and modern messaging to preserve institutional memory and open opportunities for younger generations.

Reclamation standards and Laguna Lake history: Cayetano asked whether the PRA considered reclamation acceptable when it served a public purpose, was sustainable, and followed environmental requirements. Lopez agreed, including for projects in Taguig. Lopez described reclamation as filling water to create land for a specified use; Cayetano cited the NEDA-PRA joint order and PRA rules defining the process as converting foreshore or submerged areas to land, and Lopez agreed.

During questions about the Laguna Lake area, Lopez said he was unfamiliar with several earlier road and flood-control projects. PRA Assistant General Manager Joseph John M. Literal said PRA GIS and satellite backtracking showed areas formerly submerged and now covered, from around 2000, a record Cayetano argued could not establish the earlier shoreline or historic ownership; he confirmed that titled land remains titled. Cayetano questioned how estimates of newly dry land accounted for historical dry land. Literal confirmed that earlier C6 and Manggahan-related reclamation projects had not obtained PRA permits, and agreed that such works met the PRA definition of reclamation.

PRA General Manager and CEO Cesar S. Siador Jr. said he knew the existing alignment but was not familiar with the exact area outside the dike. Cayetano cited a March 1997 flood-control project financed by a 9.411 billion yen official development assistance loan and a P14 billion allotment for a 2020 right-of-way or flyover proposal, which he said Taguig residents opposed because it would demolish more than 10,000 homes. His point was that earlier public works and erosion had to be examined before newly dry areas were described as unauthorized reclamation.

Overlapping jurisdiction and permit responsibility: Cayetano asked who had jurisdiction over Laguna Lake. Literal said PRA's jurisdiction overlapped with the Laguna Lake Development Authority (LLDA), acknowledged that LLDA had asserted exclusive jurisdiction, and answered no when asked whether PRA had respected that position. Literal referred to an Office of the Government Corporate Counsel opinion from 1995 and Department of Justice (DOJ) Opinion No. 106, series of 1995. He described the DOJ opinion as finding no substantial conflict between the cited LLDA Act and agency-charter provisions, requiring harmonization, and treating the provisions as in pari materia, with the special law an exception to the general law.

Cayetano read that explanation as placing Laguna Lake reclamation under the LLDA's special-law authority; Literal disagreed and said the opinion treated exclusive jurisdiction over the lake differently from reclamation. The exchange did not resolve their competing readings. When Cayetano asked who should obtain a permit for unauthorized reclamation and related infrastructure, Literal said the reclamation proponent should apply and identified DPWH as the proponent in the Taguig case. Cayetano said the dispute reflected a coordination and process gap among agencies, not fault by the city or himself.

PIEC's competitiveness and visitor projections: Tulfo asked what competitive advantage the Philippine International Exhibition Center (PIEC) would offer over existing convention and exhibition facilities, and why international organizers would choose it over venues in Singapore and Thailand. Lopez cited lower labor and hotel costs in the Philippines, the appeal of a new exhibition destination, and inquiries already received. He added that organizers seek variety beyond Thailand, Singapore, Japan and China.

Tulfo then asked how many international conventions, exhibitions and events the PIEC expected to attract each year and what assumptions supported the projection. Lopez said the projections drew on Department of Tourism (DOT) information and data from Singapore and Thailand. He cited 1.5 million visitors at Singapore Expo last year and a 120,000-square-meter venue; Thailand's 240,000-square-meter venue drew 1.2 million. He cautioned that those established venues were not directly comparable and said the PIEC was using a low initial projection: one or two events at roughly half the cited venue scale could bring 140,000 tourists. He estimated average visitor spending at P30,000. Lopez said the PIEC would be the largest in Southeast Asia at 243,000 square meters. These are comparative figures and projections, not reported PIEC attendance or realized visitor spending.
