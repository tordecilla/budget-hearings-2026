At a glance

  • GCG oversight: Members questioned how to assess GOCC performance beyond dividend remittances and discussed public-service subsidies, performance reporting, and the planned Integrated Corporate Reporting System.

GCG presentation

Mandate, governance results and reform priorities: The GCG described its mandate under Republic Act No. 10149, the GOCC Governance Act of 2011, as oversight of government-owned or -controlled corporations (GOCCs), including rationalizing the sector, ensuring qualified board appointments, evaluating performance and setting compensation and position-classification policies. As of June 30, 2026, it supervised 112 GOCCs across nine sectors. The sector’s average Corporate Governance Scorecard rating was 67.14%, above the 61% 2025 Agency Fiscal Targets; GCG said it assessed all GOCCs. Corpus corrected the approval date of the Compensation and Position Classification System II (CPCS II) to September 2025. No GOCC application for implementation had been received in the first half of 2026. For the 2025 talent pool, 38.25% were included in the shortlist against a 37% target. The sector’s performance-evaluation average was 75.70% against a 78% target, while all qualified GOCC submissions were subjected to performance target-setting, exceeding the stated 80% target. For 2026, scorecard validation was underway with an 80% target, dependent partly on supporting Commission on Audit (COA) and Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA) reports; 28 COA-audited reports and partial ARTA customer-satisfaction results had been received by June 30. GCG said performance-target setting begins in the third quarter and evaluation follows submission of GOCC performance documents.

Its priorities included CPCS II implementation and preparation for CPCS-3, an enhanced integrated corporate reporting and performance-evaluation system, possible dispositive action for GOCCs, the Whistleblowing and Integrity Portal, talent-pool development, liquidation of GOCCs approved for abolition, scorecard enhancement and organizational structuring.

Defective products and the pace of complaint resolution: Tulfo described complaints involving defective electronics and other products, including sellers refusing returns or offering a replacement only with an extra payment, and asked how DTI handles no-return/no-exchange policies and refund or replacement requests. DTI Assistant Secretary Atty. Regino D. Mallari Jr. said DTI’s consumer-care unit contacts merchants and attempts mediation; cases with grounds for complaint can proceed to DTI’s Adjudication Division, which assesses the facts and appropriate remedy, including return, refund or replacement.

Mallari initially put mediation at about one month. After Tulfo asked about adjudication, Mallari gave an average of around six months, sometimes less; Tulfo characterized the overall wait as roughly seven months and urged faster handling, especially for lower-cost items. He said unresolved cases should prompt steps favoring consumers. Mallari said respondents may face administrative penalties.

Mallari reported about 42,000 consumer complaints received in 2025. He later described mediation as averaging 10–15 days and said a majority settled at mediation; in response to Aquino’s request for underlying figures, he promised to submit them and estimated that 70–80% settle, with Aquino checking that this occurred within the first 10 days and Mallari agreeing. These later figures differ from Mallari’s earlier estimate of about one month for mediation.
