## Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB)

### Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB)

Teodoro framed the Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB) as coordinating prevention, treatment, rehabilitation and reintegration alongside enforcement, rather than operating the whole treatment system or acting primarily as an arresting agency. He said families may seek treatment and recovery support, and argued that a national strategy should connect separate government programs.

Teodoro cited the 2023 National Household Survey, saying current drug-use prevalence fell from 2.05% in 2019 to 1.71% in 2023, representing approximately 1.4 million people. He cautioned that needs differ: some people may require residential treatment, others outpatient or community-based rehabilitation, and others continuing support. He said data should help government identify what help is needed, where it is needed, and how people can reach it.

Teodoro said the Department of Health was best positioned to report national treatment capacity, including beds, occupancy, waiting lists, outpatient services, personnel, equipment and facility needs, while DDB should bring information together under a national strategy. He described enforcement, health and treatment, local community programs, social support and reintegration as work distributed across agencies and local governments. He also said NEP-recognized rehabilitation funding extends beyond DDB's regular appropriation through statutory funding sources involving Philippine Charity Sweepstakes Office (PCSO) and Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) revenues. He described the intended result as accessible treatment, support after rehabilitation and effective community programs.

Teodoro argued that anti-drug policy should distinguish people who profit from illegal drugs from people seeking to recover from dependence, pairing enforcement with help and a path back to the community. He concluded by urging the House to approve DDB's proposed FY2027 budget.

### Rep. Presley C. De Jesus

Rep. Presley C. De Jesus asked what the Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB) does and whether local governments were included. Sponsor Rep. Marcelino “Marcy” R. Teodoro described DDB as a coordinating and policy-formulation body implementing the Philippine Anti-Illegal Drugs Strategy. He said community-based rehabilitation involves local government units (LGUs), while DDB coordinates enforcement, prosecution, rehabilitation and reintegration agencies, including the Philippine National Police, Department of Justice, Department of Social Welfare and Development, Department of Education, Commission on Higher Education, Department of the Interior and Local Government, Department of Foreign Affairs and National Youth Commission. Teodoro said research and data gathering support evidence-based policy.

Asked about private-sector representation, he acknowledged the identified nongovernmental-organization (NGO) seat was vacant and said the board could recalibrate its position; he also identified the Integrated Bar of the Philippines president as an ex-officio member. De Jesus urged filling the NGO seat for checks and balances. Teodoro said the Commission on Human Rights was not on the board because its constitutional independence made membership difficult, while agreeing that drug policy should balance rights and health.

Asked about the Special Account in the General Fund (SAGF) for treatment and rehabilitation facilities, Teodoro reported ₱175 million obtained in 2026, including ₱60 million from the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR), ₱81 million attributed in the exchange to “PCS,” ₱3 million from Metro Manila Turf Club Corporation and ₱29 million from licenses; he gave the combined figure as ₱175,015,272.

De Jesus cited Board Resolution No. 12, Series of 2026, as approving ₱15 million for three named localities and Resolution No. 15 as approving ₱10 million for two others, then questioned a ₱50 million figure for Resolution No. 12 and the apparent ₱5 million per locality. Teodoro explained that the ₱5 million grants support facility infrastructure, while operating costs and staffing are separate and may draw on LGU and Philippine National Police resources. He said allocations reflected identified drug problems and barangay-clearing classifications.

For monitoring, he cited Commission on Audit procurement and disbursement oversight and a four-agency body involving the Department of Health, Department of the Interior and Local Government, DDB and Commission on Audit.

On recurring costs, he said services are devolved to LGUs, with the Department of Health responsible for facility accreditation and management; he acknowledged that drug rehabilitation and treatment had no dedicated budget line in the Department of Health budget he reviewed and that bed and outpatient capacity were inadequate.

De Jesus said funding for DDB advocacy and information work was described as moving from ₱27.7 million in FY2026 to ₱32.85 million in FY2027, while characterizing the change as a reduction and asking about targets, utilization and the rationale. Teodoro did not give a target or utilization rate; he explained that DDB is a coordinating agency rather than the implementing body for higher-education programs, and said its operating support focuses on research, data gathering and coordination. He described a proposed national dashboard and information portal to assemble agency data and guide policy, rather than directly implement reintegration programs. De Jesus said he supported stronger DDB funding and its advocacy.

De Jesus asked how the PHP 102.14 million FY2027 program monitoring and evaluation allocation would translate into operating platforms and participating LGUs and agencies. Teodoro described two systems: online processing of certifications and exemptions for non-controlled substances, then serving 393 establishments; and an Anti-Drug Abuse Council reporting system for screening, assessment and intervention data. He said 35 LGUs had active accounts, with training underway for 300 more, and hoped to expand participation nationwide. Version 1 launched in March and was still being updated; national treatment, rehabilitation and reintegration data remained incomplete. The exchange did not provide a count of partner agencies contributing data.

De Jesus asked why Board Regulation No. 5, Series of 2026 extended authorization for ordinary prescriptions for controlled dangerous drugs through June 30, 2027, citing the Department of Health’s failure to restore full availability of official yellow prescription forms. Teodoro said procurement was still pending and that the Department of Health had sought an eight-month period to procure and implement the forms; the extension runs for one year through June 30, 2027, with the remaining four months intended for nationwide distribution. De Jesus asked what would happen if the department missed the deadline and said he would raise the issue at its hearing. Teodoro cited the Department of Health’s role in rehabilitation and treatment, acknowledged gaps in beds and outpatient capacity and said there was no budget line for that purpose in the budget he had reviewed. The exchange records the department’s commitment and the extension, but no specific consequence for missing the deadline.

As his time ended, De Jesus said he supported DDB and offered legislative support for its budget and anti-drug work. Teodoro thanked him.

### Rep. Renee Louise M. Co

Rep. Renee Louise M. Co asked Rep. Marcelino “Marcy” R. Teodoro, the Dangerous Drugs Board (DDB) sponsor, how the DDB was implementing the Philippine Anti-Drug Strategy (PADS), when its 2018 framework would be replaced by an enhanced PADS, and whether communities and affected groups had helped shape it. Teodoro said the DDB had led implementation since the 2018 executive order; the enhanced plan was under Presidential Management Staff review, with a Palace commitment to issue it by December. In practice, he said, the DDB was already applying three pillars through a board resolution: supply reduction, demand reduction, and health, social, and developmental services, including prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and reintegration. He described recurring returns to drug use after treatment as linked to limited education and livelihood opportunities and stigma.

On consultation, Teodoro cited earlier work with the University of the Philippines Institute of Human Rights and UP College of Law, whose report had not been returned; he said the Committee on Dangerous Drugs had requested it. He also described smaller local consultations and civil-society participation, but said he would seek a list to identify who had been consulted.

Co asked for that list, the 2018 PADS, the consultation report, and progress on the enhanced plan before its expected release, so local communities, civil society, affected people and families could provide input. Teodoro agreed to ask the concerned agencies to submit the requested information and said input should come from local communities and people.

Co asked how the DDB guided anti-drug budgeting across agencies and whether the stated policy shift was reflected in allocations. Teodoro said the DDB board meets with representatives of 12 line agencies; a technical committee presents a proposal for collective approval according to priorities. He said the DDB used National Household Survey data on drug use and addiction, including age, demographics and location, to guide policy and allocation. He also identified PHP 111.69 million for surveys, research and policy formulation; PHP 169.18 million for prevention and control; and PHP 24 million for capacity building, human-rights training and advocacy. Later, he said supply reduction represented 42% against 57% for demand reduction, describing this as a gradual budget shift corresponding to policy. He added that DDB sought to connect people with services from the Department of Health (DOH), Department of Education (DepEd), Commission on Higher Education (CHED), Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD), and other agencies through its coordinating role and interdepartmental board representation.

Co asked how a health-first, rights-based strategy would change spending across DDB and the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG), Department of Justice (DOJ), and Bureau of Jail Management and Penology (BJMP), including funding for alternatives to incarceration. She cited reports that drug-related cases accounted for 90% of people in detention, 80% of prosecutors' dockets, and about 70% of prior-year anti-drug campaign spending was for public order and law enforcement; she argued that policy change should redirect resources toward health and rights.

Teodoro agreed that the strategy should balance supply and demand reduction with health and social services. He said the DDB conducts research, coordinates with the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE), CHED, DSWD and DOH, and had raised the shortage of treatment facilities with DOH; an online access system was intended to help people locate assistance. He said the DDB was also speaking with DOJ about plea bargaining, while stressing that the purpose was not simply to reduce jail populations and describing concerns about people being falsely accused in police operations.

Teodoro cited the Philippine Amusement and Gaming Corporation (PAGCOR) statutory contribution as reaching 14 areas at PHP 5 million per local government, and questioned whether new rehabilitation centers would have doctors and social workers. Sponsor Rep. Julienne "Jam" L. Erice questioned whether funding matched the scale of DHSUD's urban-development responsibilities. Baronda said comprehensive land-use planning (CLUP) was a policy function for local government units (LGUs) to implement, and that DHSUD had no funds for its local implementation. She said more than 36 LGUs had begun CLUP work and that the guidelines were being integrated into local plans; compliance and implementation reporting remained part of Erice's request.

Erice questioned the original 4PH goal of one million housing units a year against a stated housing backlog of 6.5 million units, and cited more than 575,000 units facilitated against a recalibrated target of about 1.13 million, or 51%. He asked whether the revised target reflected a realistic assessment of housing need and asked for delivery status rather than counting all facilitated units alike. Baronda said the program had only one modality at first and that weak LGU participation and delayed permitting were significant problems.

When Erice asked how many units were completed, occupied, under construction or only approved, and which beneficiaries they reached, Baronda promised a detailed report. Her preliminary figures shifted between 50,000 and 11,000, then 50,000 and 11,700, with an approximate total of 69,000, and she distinguished financing/home loans from construction without clarifying all categories.
