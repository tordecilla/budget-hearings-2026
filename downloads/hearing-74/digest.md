## At a glance

- **Commission on Audit (COA) and audit capacity:** Members examined COA’s ability to expand audits, modernize its systems, retain specialists and extend audit coverage.

- **Commission on Elections (COMELEC) and election readiness:** COMELEC presented election preparations; members questioned voting-machine procurement, online voting education and overseas voter access.

- **Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA) and public services:** The ARTA exchanges covered regulatory compliance, regional capacity, complaint handling, digital systems and the effects of reduced operating support.

- **Anti-Money Laundering Council (AMLC), safeguards and recovery:** AMLC interpellations covered risk-proportionate safeguards for nonprofit organizations, confidentiality, online sexual abuse and exploitation of children reporting, freeze orders, forfeitures, and whether frozen assets had actually been recovered.

- **National Intelligence Coordinating Agency (NICA):** NICA’s sponsor briefly presented the proposed budget and offered to answer questions; no substantive interpellation followed.

- **National Security Council (NSC), policy and accountability:** Members questioned the NSC journal’s peer review, authorship and treatment of civic activity; they raised rights, privacy and human-security concerns and debated alliances, defense posture, staffing and confidential spending.

- **National Amnesty Commission (NAC):** Members questioned slow amnesty processing, the gap between applications and presidential grants, and administrative constraints; the sponsor outlined remedies without a firm completion timetable.

- **Office of the Presidential Adviser on Peace, Reconciliation and Unity (OPAPRU):** OPAPRU interpellations covered PAMANA implementation and audits, normalization, education assistance, transitional justice, and women’s participation and security.

### Commission on Audit (COA)

FY 2027 proposed budget and request. The Commission on Audit (COA) sponsor, Rep. John Tracy F. Cagas, presented a total of ₱16.46 billion including automatic appropriations; new appropriations subject to congressional approval were ₱14.774 billion. He compared the latter with a FY 2026 level of ₱14.415 billion, an increase he stated as ₱359.12 million or about 2.49%. In an interpellation, Cagas separately described COA’s original pre-NEP proposal as ₱17 billion and the FY 2027 National Expenditure Program (NEP) amount as ₱15 billion, with ₱2 billion intended for technology not provided. These figures are not reconciled with the presentation’s total and new-appropriation figures. Rep. Antonio L. Tinio separately referred to a ₱14.7 billion proposed budget.

Components and restoration. Cagas listed FY 2027 maintenance and other operating expenses (MOOE) for the Information Systems Strategic Plan at ₱213.686 million and Citizen Participatory Audit participation by nongovernmental and people’s organizations at ₱40.734 million. He gave an unclear MOOE phrase, “P254 million and P420 pesos,” and later restated the combined amount as “244?254 million 120,000”; the exact total remains unresolved. For capital outlay he listed ₱484.617 million for buildings and structures, ₱161.523 million for furniture and fixtures, ₱5.6 million for transportation equipment and ₱525.405 million for information and communications technology equipment and software; he stated a ₱1.177145 billion total not included in the NEP and confirmed COA lacked funding for that equipment. He said the gap would delay technology adoption. Rep. Percival “Perci” Cendaña supported restoring the reduced capital outlay, and Rep. Tobias “Toby” M. Tiangco expressed full support for COA’s budget; neither support nor the interpellation termination records a restoration disposition.

### Commission on Elections (COMELEC)

FY 2027 proposed budget and election preparation. The Commission on Elections (COMELEC) sponsor, Rep. Keith Micah “Atty. Mike” D.L. Tan, presented a proposed FY 2027 NEP budget of ₱29.5 billion including automatic appropriations, ₱9.31 billion or 46 percent above the FY 2026 General Appropriations Act (GAA) level of ₱20.2 billion. Voting-machine request and restoration. During interpellation, Rep. Edgar R. Erice cited a ₱23 billion request for voting machines; Tan described the request at about ₱23.1 billion and later as ₱23.16 billion, while citing ₱21.66 billion in the current budget and stating that the FY 2027 proposal was ₱16 billion and the FY 2027 NEP was ₱21.66 billion. The exchange records advocacy and support, not a formal motion or appropriation disposition.

### Office of the Presidential Adviser on Peace, Reconciliation and Unity (OPAPRU)

FY 2027 proposed NEP budget: ₱9.3 billion for the Office of the Presidential Adviser on Peace, Reconciliation and Unity (OPAPRU). No original agency-request total was stated. Rep. Elijah Rumbaoa San Fernando described a ₱1.171 billion, or 14.4 percent, increase; the exchange does not establish this as a separate augmentation on top of ₱9.3 billion. San Fernando described a ₱6.090 billion Payapa at Masaganang Pamayanan (PAMANA) request within the proposed budget.

PAMANA allocation and components. Rep. Dadah Kiram Ismula referred to ₱6.90 billion for FY 2027 PAMANA projects; Rep. Jose “Joboy” S. Aquino II later stated a PAMANA target budget of ₱6,090,290,000. The source does not reconcile these figures. Rep. Renee Louise M. Co cited the proposed allocation as ₱6.09 billion, with ₱3.76 billion for roads, ₱807 million for educational assistance, ₱377 million for community infrastructure, ₱375 million for bridges and ₱258 million for social protection. For 25 Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) projects, Aquino’s amount was fragmented as “800,” then “868 million 440,000”; it is not combined into a total. He described ₱563 million for 23 Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF) normalization projects and ₱2.2 billion for normalization, including camps and the Camps Transformation Program; the answer gave no amount for seven Moro National Liberation Front (MNLF) projects. He separately described about ₱1.4 billion for projects implemented directly by the Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD), National Commission on Indigenous Peoples (NCIP) and Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth) through a FY 2027 NEP special provision, separate from PAMANA.

PAMANA audit and implementation. San Fernando cited COA findings of ₱1.26 billion unliquidated in the second quarter of 2026, ₱3.93 billion unliquidated at 2025 year-end, and ₱9.936 billion in total unliquidated PAMANA transfers. Aquino answered with a 2017 transfer total spoken as “6 billion, 185 million, 513,” whose final unit is unclear. For July 2024, he gave a fragmented sequence, “3 billion, 698, 635, 631,” in describing amounts liquidated and refunded, without making clear whether it is one or two totals. He said corresponding liquidation reports had been sent to COA, that OPAPRU had reactivated citizen-led monitoring, and that the Secretary does not approve questionable projects; he invited members to visit and geotag projects.

Education assistance and other peace-process work. Aquino stated ₱52 million for program-management support, separately from unclear project-target figures rendered as “258 million students” for social protection and “832 million 290” for education assistance. He later identified ₱850 million as the current-year educational-assistance amount. He said assistance was not provided in 2023, 2024 or 2025; for 2026, he said OPAPRU had received no Department of Budget and Management (DBM) cash download, no funds had been released, and utilization was zero. He said the agency was working on MOAs with state universities. In a later exchange he said universities with MOAs provide ₱25,000 per semester. Co separately cited educational-assistance figures of ₱807 million, ₱807,500,000 and ₱807,000,000; the exchange does not reconcile them.

For FY 2027, Aquino said ₱66 million was allotted to the Transitional Justice and Reconciliation Roadmap: ₱14 million for Track 1 to establish the proposed transitional-justice body and ₱51 million for Track 2 support. The components total ₱65 million; the source does not explain the difference. The work covered truth, history, justice, reparations and guarantees, including a land inventory in six MILF camps and work with Congress on the proposed bill. He separately said ₱100 million was required to fulfill the roadmap, without identifying it as a formal additional request. Aquino stated a FY 2027 Women, Peace and Security allocation of ₱12,195,000 for partner capacity-building, collaboration and resource mobilization, localization and mainstreaming, monitoring and evaluation, and unit administration. The H74 OPAPRU interpellation period was terminated; no appropriation disposition for the cited requests or increase is recorded.

### Commission on Audit (COA)

Cagas described the Commission on Audit (COA) as the country’s supreme auditing institution and guardian of the public treasury. The exchange identifies technology as a possible condition for more frequent barangay audits, without a specific implementation commitment.

Montes asked how COA develops auditors’ competence and integrity and whether specialized training carries a service obligation. Cagas said training begins when employees enter COA and continues through their careers. Montes then asked whether COA had funds and staffing to recruit engineers, information-technology specialists and other professionals able to assess complex audits, including infrastructure pricing. Cagas said COA wanted specialists but had limited plantilla positions, including for engineers. ### Rep. Sarah Jane I. Elago

Voter registration and overseas voting. Rep. Sarah Jane I. Elago of Gabriela Women’s Partylist referred to the Commission on Elections (COMELEC) goal, presented at its FY 2027 budget briefing, of increasing registered voters from 1.2 million to 2 million before the 2028 national elections. In September 2026, she asked how many voters had been added and how many were overseas. Rep. Keith Micah “Atty. Mike” D.L. Tan, the recognized sponsor, reported about 1,014,701 registered overseas voters as of August 12, 2026, and hoped the count would grow by 500,000 before the next national and local election. He did not give an overall net increase in registered voters.

Elago raised the challenge of voter awareness among Filipinos working or living overseas and asked whether registration could also enroll voters in the new online voting system. Tan said COMELEC was studying that suggestion and described town hall meetings with Filipino community leaders, expanded education on the full online voting process, planned mock voting and system demonstrations, easier enrollment, and help desks and feedback channels through COMELEC, the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) and community partners. Elago welcomed coordination that would let overseas Filipino workers register before departure and combine registration with enrollment.

Elago said internet voting was planned at 104 posts for the 2028 national elections and asked whether voting would be fully online rather than by post or in person. Tan confirmed COMELEC planned fully online voting for 2028. Elago raised the need for more public information and the burden of a separate enrollment step. Tan said the voting and counting system was still undergoing its terms-of-reference process and that COMELEC was refining it for usability, vote security and voter confidence. Elago said she was awaiting COMELEC’s assessment of the online system first used in 2025 and noted that bills under House deliberation would preserve options for voting by post or in person alongside online voting.

Bangsamoro election reports and safeguards. Elago said her questions about the September 14, 2026 Bangsamoro parliamentary election drew on reports from civil-society organizations accredited by COMELEC as local observers. She asked what action followed reports that some voters in Maluso, Basilan, received ballots already marked or pre-shaded. Tan said COMELEC itself discovered the pre-shading and recalled the affected ballots. He said the Commission was tracing possible tampering through the chain of custody and had taken a further step the previous day concerning those potentially responsible, though the type of action is unclear in the transcript. Asked how many reports were under verification, Tan said approximately 10 reports were being verified. Elago asked that an initial report be submitted to the House Committee on Appropriations and sought transparent, timely updates for complainants. Tan said the Commission was open to updates and asked that incidents be reported promptly so it could act.

Elago also asked why military and uniformed personnel were deployed in some areas and how COMELEC would ensure their presence did not intimidate voters or restrict free voting. Tan said COMELEC focused on “red category” areas with a history of election violence. He cited an 84% turnout in the Bangsamoro as evidence of peaceful voting and said the data showed police had helped voters cast ballots freely. Elago said she would review the Commission’s initial report to assess whether voting had been free and the election fair and clean.

## Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA)

### Anti-Red Tape Authority (ARTA)

Sagarbarria described ARTA’s role in reducing regulatory burdens, streamlining government processes, and encouraging agencies to use more efficient, digitalized systems. She said the proposed initiatives included enhancements to digital platforms, the Philippine-based regulation information system, electronic complaints management, and other digital programs intended to make government transactions more accessible, transparent, and efficient. ### Rep. Iris Marie Demesa Montes

Montes asked how many red-tape complaints ARTA received in 2025 and how many it resolved within the required period. Sagarbarria answered with figures “as of 2026”: 12,335 cases received and 11,208 resolved within the turnaround time, or 90.86%. The answer therefore supplies a 2026 tally rather than a separately identified 2025 total.

Asked which offices drew the most complaints, Sagarbarria gave separate top-ten lists. Among government-owned and controlled corporations, she named the Home Development Mutual Fund (Pag-IBIG Fund), Social Security System, Government Service Insurance System, Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth), Land Bank of the Philippines, Philippine Postal Corporation, National Transmission Corporation, National Food Authority, Intellectual Property Office and Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority; she identified Pag-IBIG as the highest. For national government agencies, she named the Land Transportation Office, Bureau of Internal Revenue, Food and Drug Administration, Philippine Statistics Authority, National Telecommunications Commission, Department of Education, Land Registration Authority, Register of Deeds, Professional Regulation Commission and Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development. For local governments, she listed Quezon City, Taguig, Caloocan, Pasig, Parañaque, Malolos, Manila, San Jose del Monte, Makati, Muntinlupa and a further city whose name is unclear in the transcript.

Montes asked whether agencies had public assistance and complaint desks and whether they were properly staffed. Sagarbarria said agencies, rather than ARTA, must provide the personnel. After Montes described a help desk in a top-complaint agency with no staff, Sagarbarria acknowledged her frustration, said ARTA conducts compliance inspections, apologized for what had happened and said inspections would be conducted in the future to verify that desks are actually staffed.

On agency Committees on Anti-Red Tape (CARTs), Sagarbarria reported 70% compliance and said noncompliant agencies would receive notices to explain. She said 98% complied with the Citizens’ Charter requirement; in response to Montes’s question about whether agencies followed the charters in practice, she said ARTA requires annual compliance updates and also conducts inspections.

Montes asked whether ARTA investigations led to cases or accountability. Sagarbarria reported 600 investigations and 373 cases filed, but did not give case dispositions or penalties. Asked how many agencies received ARTA recommendations and how many acted on them, she described ongoing whole-of-government reengineering of systems and procedures without providing the requested counts.

As an example of process simplification, Sagarbarria said telecommunications permits were reduced from 13 to 8 and documentary requirements from 86 to 35. Processing time fell from 241 days to 16 days. She said approved telecommunications permit applications rose from 6,117 to 10,465 as of June 2026, while fixed-internet download speed increased from 93.96 Mbps to 102.79 Mbps in December 2025.

Asked how ARTA measures whether red tape is declining and clients are more satisfied, Sagarbarria described ARTA’s Report Card Survey. She said it uses feedback from actual clients to assess compliance with the Citizens’ Charter and service-delivery requirements, and uses random sampling and validation of agency reports to identify improvements. She also cited an ARTA report card and a client-satisfaction measurement, but did not provide a trend in complaints or dissatisfaction that directly answered Montes’s question about whether cases or dissatisfied clients had declined.

Montes asked Sagarbarria to rate ARTA’s performance since its 2018 establishment under Republic Act No. 11032. Sagarbarria rated the agency 9 out of 10. Montes called that a self-rating, said the work remained in progress, expressed full support for ARTA’s budget, and challenged the Authority to continue improving government transactions.
