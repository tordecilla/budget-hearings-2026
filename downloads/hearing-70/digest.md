## Agency budget proposals

### Office of the President (OP)

For fiscal year 2027, the Office of the President (OP) proposed National Expenditure Program (NEP) allocation was ₱10,155,758,000, inclusive of retirement and life insurance premiums; a separate original request to the Department of Budget and Management (DBM) was not stated. House Committee on Appropriations Senior Vice Chair Representative Albert S. Garcia presented it as about 0.14% of the ₱7.2 trillion national budget. By allotment class, the proposal comprised ₱7.46 billion for maintenance and other operating expenses (74%), ₱1.86 billion for personal services (18%), and ₱839.66 million for capital outlay (8%). By program, general administration and support received ₱2.74 billion and operations ₱7.41 billion; the four operations programs were Presidential Oversight (₱5.37 billion), Executive Staff Service (₱1.63 billion), Advisory (₱184.6 million) and Legal and Legislative Services (₱180 million). The special provision requires quarterly financial and physical reports within 30 days after each quarter, online posting, and notice to both houses and their appropriations and finance committees.

On the sponsor’s comparable basis, excluding retirement and life insurance premiums and pension, the FY2026 allocation of ₱28.03 billion fell to ₱10.08 billion in FY2027, a stated ₱17.95 billion (64.02%) decrease. Garcia attributed about ₱17.55 billion to completed FY2026 locally funded projects, principally Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) 2026, and a further ₱392.52 million (3.75%) regular-program trim. The separate FY2027 NEP proposal should not be conflated with a distinct original agency request.

The confidential and intelligence funds (CIF) exchange addressed several scopes. Representative Antonio L. Tinio cited proposed FY2027 government-wide CIF of ₱10.77 billion and OP CIF of about ₱4.5 billion, or 45.22% of the OP FY2027 budget. His historical comparisons were government-wide CIF below ₱2 billion in 2001–2016, almost ₱6 billion in 2017 and ₱12.120 billion in 2025; for the OP he cited ₱2.8 billion through 2016 and a 54% share of its budget in 2020. Garcia described the aggregate under discussion as ₱11 billion plus, about 0.15% of the national budget; the exchange does not reconcile that description with Tinio’s separate agency and government-wide figures. Tinio also cited a prior Department of Justice (DOJ) disclosure he described as a ₱700 million CIF breakdown: ₱500 million for witness protection, ₱10 million for the Interagency Council Against Trafficking, ₱475,000 for the Office of Cybercrime and ₱5 million for a center he described as addressing online sexual abuse or exploitation of children.

The Local Government Support Fund (LGSF), distinct from the OP’s own allocation, was compared at ₱16.08 billion in the FY2026 NEP and ₱57.8 billion in the FY2026 General Appropriations Act (GAA); the FY2027 proposed NEP amount was ₱58.5 billion, with no separate original request stated. Tinio cited reported releases of ₱500 million to Naga City (later described as more than ₱500 million), ₱500 million for a Lipa City multipurpose building and ₱30 million or less for most Financial Assistance to Local Government Units recipients; these were his distribution examples. Representative Renee Louise M. Co characterized Barangay Development Program support as ₱5 million per barangay while challenging its safeguards; Garcia promised documentation, without confirming a separate appropriation in that exchange. Representative Sarah Jane I. Elago asked whether the ₱39 million in FY2027 maintenance and other operating expenses for the Office of the Presidential Adviser for Marawi Rehabilitation and Development (OPA-MRD) covered consultation with displaced Marawi residents and requested a breakdown; Garcia gave the amount, and no breakdown was supplied in that exchange. A ₱125 billion FY2027 block grant for the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) was also discussed, outside the OP allocation.

Representative Edgar R. Erice, said the Office of the President’s proposed budget represented a 64% reduction from its 2026 allocation and considered a ₱4 billion intelligence and confidential fund justified in light of the West Philippine Sea situation.

### Commission on Higher Education (CHED)

The Commission on Higher Education (CHED) FY2027 National Expenditure Program (NEP) proposal was ₱35.45 billion, inclusive of automatic appropriations, compared by the sponsor with a current FY2026 level of ₱48.27 billion; she described the decrease as ₱12.9 billion or 26.7%. A separate original agency request to the Department of Budget and Management (DBM) was not stated. Representative Renee Louise M. Co asked about CHED’s formula for subsidies to State Universities and Colleges (SUCs) and local universities and colleges (LUCs). Co relayed DBM’s description of a revised method using projected enrollment and average growth over the prior three years. Sponsor Representative Monique Yazmin Maria Q. Lagdameo described a study attributed to SGV intended to provide a standard measurable basis, with cost of attendance and faculty-to-student ratios included. Lagdameo promised Co the study, CHED’s normative-budget computation and requested information. Current Free Higher Education (FHE) rates were ₱5,565 per SUC student per semester and ₱7,154 per LUC student per semester.

Co attributed a historical ₱12,307,136,000 deficiency across 113 SUCs to erroneous past calculations and said it was nearly fully addressed. Lagdameo reported claims for academic years 2022–23, 2023–24 and 2024–25 were available for pickup by 79 of 108 SUCs, with compliance still required for others; because the prior special provision ended with 2024–25, the first semester of 2025–26 was excluded. For remaining gaps, Lagdameo estimated ₱5 billion for FY2025–26 and ₱2.8 billion for FY2027. Co recalled an earlier ₱15 billion funding call and later supported ₱4,971,881,000 for FY2025–26 and ₱2,766,216,000 for the projected FY2027 gap; these were supported requests, not adopted appropriations.

For standardizing SUC subsidy fees, Lagdameo described alternative estimates of ₱53.7 billion in the study attributed to SGV and ₱29.6 billion in the DBM estimate, separate from the ₱4.230 billion CHED leveling-up option to set ₱250 per unit for 106 SUCs below that threshold. A separate 5% option for seven other SUCs was estimated at ₱87.4 million. Representative Antonio L. Tinio supported leveling up; no option was adopted in the exchange. He also proposed an initial 0.1% budget share for persons with disabilities across agencies, using a ₱7.2 trillion national budget and stating a resulting ₱7.2 billion; this remained a proposal. Representative Dadah Kiram Ismula cited a FY2027 curriculum-integration request; its precise amount is unclear. Representative Percival “Perci” Cendaña asked that the Philippine General Hospital (PGH) FY2027 allocation be restored to its FY2026 General Appropriations Act (GAA) level after a stated ₱805 million reduction; the request was left for consideration.

## Office of the President (OP)

### Rep. Renee Louise M. Co

Barangay Development Program eligibility, safeguards and per-barangay funding. Rep. Renee Louise M. Co, the interpellator, asked how the Office of the President (OP) determines which barangays qualify for the Barangay Development Program (BDP), given that the President chairs the National Task Force to End Local Communist Armed Conflict (NTF-ELCAC). Rep. Albert S. Garcia, the OP sponsor, said local peace and order councils, local governments and national agencies validate an area’s insurgency-free status from the ground up, with referrals proceeding through regional and national levels before the barangay becomes eligible for BDP support. He listed completion of the Community Support Program (CSP) and Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG)’s Retooled Community Support Program (RCSP), a functional Integrated Territorial Defense System (ITDS) and barangay institutions, and an implemented three-year Peace and Order and Public Safety plan. He said the barangay development council formulates the plan with the community, with support from the local and national governments. Garcia described the approach as holistic, covering economic and social development alongside peace and order.

Co pressed for operational criteria beyond frameworks and signatures, including how national agencies verify clearances and how plans and barangay security systems work in practice. She questioned whether the process could amount to automatic approval, and argued that roads and schools should be funded through agencies with those mandates. She also raised reports of student leaders being questioned about political views and organizations, warning that conditional BDP support could infringe constitutional rights and freedoms. Garcia said the listed parameters were the process and promised to submit further documentation on their on-the-ground operation. Co said her concern remained that the conditions could impair those rights.

Critical minerals agreement and Executive Order 122. Co then questioned the February memorandum of understanding (MOU) on critical minerals between the Philippines and the United States and its relationship to Executive Order 122 establishing a Critical Minerals Framework. She asked why the Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) secretary signed the MOU and whether the President’s Office cleared DENR to enter it. Garcia said the President signed the executive order and DENR’s secretary signed the MOU because mining falls under the department’s jurisdiction; he confirmed presidential clearance and characterized the MOU as exploratory and non-committal.

Garcia said the Executive’s position was that processing minerals domestically could add value to raw materials and bring higher value and more revenue to the country. Co supported mining serving the public and national interest but argued that the arrangement could prioritize opportunities for the United States, including a first opportunity to develop resources. Garcia restated the Executive’s expected public benefit. Co maintained her criticism of the MOU and its implementation through the executive order, then yielded the remaining time without further questions.

### Rep. Sarah Jane I. Elago

Fuel-tax relief and cost pressures. Representative Sarah Jane I. Elago of Gabriela Women’s Party asked why the administration had suspended excise taxes on liquefied petroleum gas and kerosene while leaving taxes on diesel, gasoline and other petroleum products in place. She cited the law’s emergency authority when Dubai crude reaches US$80 per barrel. Sponsor Representative Albert S. Garcia said the Executive was awaiting a Department of Finance (DOF) recommendation and expected it within days. Elago said subsidies did not reach everyone and were insufficient, and urged use of the emergency power for all petroleum products, consideration of value-added tax relief on essentials, and passage of a price-reduction bill.

Minimum wage and family living costs. Elago questioned whether the OP would endorse a national minimum wage tied to a living wage. Garcia offered to submit a chart comparing minimum-wage increases with inflation, saying wages had risen more and that the gap had widened over time. Elago remained concerned that minimum wages were below the poverty threshold she said had been reported by the Department of Economy, Planning, and Development (DepDev) and the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA). She renewed the call for a national minimum wage aligned with living wages, which she put at ₱1,200 to ₱1,300 per day.

Humanitarian clemency and migrant-worker protection. Elago asked what concrete steps the OP was taking to secure Mary Jane Veloso’s release, noting her prolonged separation from her family and describing her case as trafficking and forced drug-mule exploitation. Marawi rehabilitation, housing and community consultation. Elago asked whether OPA-MRD had consolidated implementing-agency reports on program status, delays, catch-up plans and assets, particularly for permanent housing and return. Garcia said a consolidated report existed and would be submitted.

Elago also asked about the terms and documents for the housing arrangement involving the Marawi Compensation Board and National Housing Authority for claimants in four barangays, and sought clearer guidance on claimant participation and compensation or reparation. ### Rep. Antonio L. Tinio

LPG emergency purchase, sale, and remaining stock. Representative Antonio L. Tinio asked about DOE’s emergency purchase of 21,000 liters of LPG. Sponsor Representative Arnie B. Fuentebella said it had been sold, mostly to LPG companies: he cited a purchase price of ₱70 per liter and sale at ₱53 per liter. Fuentebella said the transaction stabilized supply; Tinio asked whether it reduced retail prices. Tinio cited an August report showing about 11,000 liters still in storage, while Fuentebella said that stock had since been sold, including another 11,000 liters in August, and none remained.

Diesel and gasoline disposition and policy limits. Tinio cited submitted data as showing 268,180,012 liters of diesel and gasoline purchased, with 181.7 thousand liters still in storage as of August; elsewhere in the exchange, the remaining stock is described as 181 million liters. The different figures and units are not reconciled in the exchange. Fuentebella clarified that the fuel was not purchased from Russia, but through traders, and said private-sector sales followed Singapore MOPS pricing; he said the government’s objective was securing supply, not assuring lower retail prices. He said DOE was constrained from directly selling fuel to consumers and that the Department of Transportation implements fuel subsidies, while economic managers make broader executive decisions on relief. DOE would dispose of the remaining stock prudently in light of domestic and world markets to avoid a supply shortage.

Missionary fuel and strategic storage security. Tinio cited a report that 6.4 million liters of diesel had been sold to the National Power Corporation (NAPOCOR) for off-grid missionary generation at a preferential ₱80 per liter. Fuentebella distinguished those sales from private-sector sales, which followed Singapore MOPS prices. Tinio raised security concerns about the privately owned Subic storage facility, alleging that it was collocated with U.S. Navy fuel reserves; Fuentebella said DOE had not known that detail, had selected Subic because it considered the facility secure and had no state-owned depot, and recommended building a government depot while noting the current rent cost.

Visayas outages and proposed system response. Tinio cited DOE data showing 92 days of yellow alerts and 29 days of red alerts in the Visayas through early September, and described rotating outages he had experienced. Fuentebella acknowledged plant outages and outlined DOE’s response: investigating and auditing plants declaring forced outages, returning units to service on schedule, deploying 253 MW of battery storage across Cebu, Panay, Leyte, and Negros, enforcing outstanding ancillary-services contracts, monitoring grid conditions, and using temporary power barges or generators. On additional Visayas capacity, he cited 585 MW of nonrenewable capacity described as immediate, a 500 MW renewable figure, and a longer-term renewable target of 15,101 MW; he promised a detailed list by area. Tinio argued that repeated outages and prices undermined EPIRA’s promises and called for repealing the law and restoring a direct government role; this was Tinio’s position, not an adopted decision in the exchange.

### Rep. Sergio C. Dagooc

Power-demand projections and coordination. Representative Sergio C. Dagooc challenged the Department of Energy’s (DOE) Philippine Energy Plan projections, saying reliance on historical distribution-utility data can miss new resorts, businesses and other major loads. He proposed formal coordination with the Department of Trade and Industry, the Department of the Interior and Local Government, businesses and local government units so proposed developments are reflected in utility forecasts and supply plans. DOE sponsor Representative Arnie B. Fuentebella said the Department saw no problem in the plan but was open to suggestions; he agreed with Dagooc’s coordination proposal.

Generator reserves and ancillary services. Dagooc said a grid can lose contracted ancillary support when the same generating plant providing it has a forced outage. He proposed requiring generating companies to maintain their own ancillary reserves or provide cold, dispatchable reserve capacity that runs when another plant fails. Fuentebella agreed with the proposal, saying it would support transparency and accountability.

Renewable targets, system costs and public expectations. Fuentebella said the renewable generation-mix target was 50% by 2040. Dagooc supported the target but urged DOE to balance reliability and least cost and to manage public expectations: lower renewable generation prices can be offset by transmission upgrades and backup ancillary services, with costs passed to consumers. He contrasted this with rooftop solar, which does not require the same grid upgrades, and asked DOE to revisit its policy messaging. Fuentebella said DOE would revisit the policy.

Reserve-market rules and gaming concerns. Dagooc said ancillary-service procurement costs were falling while reserve-market costs were rising. He described a possible incentive for generators to terminate contracted arrangements and offer capacity into the reserve market, but said intent would be difficult to prove and framed this as a concern based on the trend, not a proven finding. He asked DOE to review the reserve-market circular and suggested considering whether NGCP could instead procure shortfalls from the regular market. Fuentebella agreed to revisit the circular and ancillary-service rules.

Dependable generation and transmission capacity inventories. Dagooc asked DOE to inventory installed and dependable generation separately, especially in the Visayas and Mindanao, and to map transmission capacity that can absorb new renewable generation without upgrades. He proposed directing developers toward locations with available capacity. Fuentebella first answered yes and, when Dagooc pressed him, said DOE was already doing this work.

Public generation and EPIRA amendment. Dagooc argued that Section 47 of EPIRA left the government dependent on private generators for ancillary services. He urged an amendment allowing at least one government-owned plant in each main grid to supply generation and ancillary capacity, built with a one-time public subsidy, with operations and maintenance recovered through paid ancillary services. Fuentebella said he totally agreed with the proposal and was supportive of Dagooc’s recommendations.

Benchmark-based VAT proposal. Dagooc said applying VAT to electricity and fuel costs can amplify price increases because the tax is applied across multiple stages of supply. He proposed setting a benchmark for each technology and taxing the benchmark amount while exempting price increases above it, preserving the government’s expected benchmark-based revenue while limiting inflationary pass-through. Fuentebella said he personally agreed with the proposal but noted it was outside DOE’s mandate.

Total-electrification alternatives. Dagooc said he would submit proposed special provisions for consideration at the proper time, including a provision allowing the National Electrification Administration to use forms of total electrification beyond line extensions. He proposed battery storage for communities with dedicated charging, and requiring storage facilities to have their own solar or wind supply so they would not add load to the transmission system. These were proposals for future consideration; this exchange records no adoption.
