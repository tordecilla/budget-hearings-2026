## At a glance

- **Justice and enforcement capacity:** Subcommittee G chair Senator Francis Kiko Pangilinan and Senators Panfilo Lacson and Joseph Victor "JV" G. Ejercito pressed Secretary Fredderick A. Vida and officials of the Department of Justice (DOJ) and its attached agencies on whether resources could support effective prosecutions, investigations, victim protection and fair treatment in custody. They highlighted staffing and equipment constraints at the National Bureau of Investigation (NBI), online child-protection funding, and agricultural-smuggling case-building. The Bureau of Immigration (BI) explained the express-lane split, while the NBI requested higher agent hazard pay and Lacson identified its statutory basis. Members also examined immigration-system phases and the proposed Civil Aviation and Immigration Security Services partnership; BI said the proposal remained under review, with no final decision. The restoration and augmentation proposals discussed across the hearing were not recorded as approved appropriations.

- **Corrections, food systems and plenary conditions:** DOJ and Bureau of Corrections (BuCor) officials discussed a stepwise transfer of the Bureau of Jail Management and Penology (BJMP) toward DOJ, with a technical working group with the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG) preparing a draft executive order and provincial-jail responsibilities identified as a legal issue. Senators also examined direct purchasing from farmers and fisherfolk, food procurement and agricultural-smuggling enforcement. DOJ agreed to provide input at a separate meeting before plenary. Pangilinan said he would endeavor to endorse restoration requests and deemed the budget submitted for plenary consideration subject to the requested information and separate meeting; this was not an appropriation approval.

Ejercito asked about the proposed Civil Aviation and Immigration Security Services (CAIS) public-private partnership (PPP), including its purpose, proponent and estimated PHP 10.7 billion project cost. Viado stressed that CAIS remained under review and that no final decision had been made; support for border-security modernization did not mean automatic approval. He said the proponent still had to justify its fee, technology, financial structure and commercial terms, and that BI, the Department of Justice (DOJ), the Department of Finance and the PPP Center would independently assess whether the proposal was necessary, reasonable and in the public interest.

Viado described CAIS as an integrated system combining advanced passenger information, biometrics, automated immigration processing, threat analytics and command functions, with technology refreshes every four years over 20 years. He estimated PHP 10.7 billion in initial private-proponent spending, PHP 116 billion in operating costs over 20 years, and PHP 52.2 billion for hardware upgrades over that period. He said the system would connect existing systems and use passenger manifests and relevant databases, including INTERPOL, to assess travelers before they reached the border. Viado said BI itself would incur no expense under the proposal.

For recovery of the proponent's costs, Viado described a proposed service fee of US$4 per travel, or US$8 for a round trip, He separately quoted a little over PHP 240 for the proposed per-travel charge. Viado said the proposal and fee remained under evaluation and technical review by BI, DOJ and the PPP Center, with public consultation still to be arranged.

### Prosecution success, convictions and evidence-standard reforms

Pangilinan distinguished prosecution success from conviction rates. He cited DOJ figures of 91% prosecution success and 59% convictions for 2026, and 90.8% success and 44.7% convictions for 2025, then asked how conviction rates could improve. He cited international comparisons of 80% to 90%, Japan at about 90% or more, and Singapore at 95%, while explicitly saying the figures came from AI and needed validation. These were his attributed comparisons, not verified benchmarks.

Vida reported that in 2025 courts decided 404,124 cases, including 180,000 convictions (44%), 18,234 acquittals, 23,017 successfully mediated matters, 30,000 archived cases and 14,000 transferred for jurisdiction. He described 56% as disposed by the courts while separating convictions from other outcomes. For the first half of 2026, he said the conviction rate had risen from 44.75% to 59.21%, almost 60%, following DOJ's higher filing standard: prima facie evidence with reasonable certainty of conviction. Vida said stronger screening reduced cases filed in court, dismissed cases that could not be won, and improved access to legal services by preventing people without effective counsel from being subjected to weak cases.

Fadullon said the higher evidence standard was introduced in 2023, so most cases in the historical figures had been filed under the former probable-cause standard. He described earlier prosecutor-law-enforcement collaboration at the start of a case, including National Bureau of Investigation (NBI), Philippine National Police (PNP), Philippine Drug Enforcement Agency (PDEA), Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) and Bureau of Customs (BOC), as necessary to build stronger cases; he said the process was still adjusting and coordination could improve.
